How the graph is built

Turning the whole cube in your hands never makes a new state. Counting every way of holding it, there are 88,179,840 arrangements, but each state appears in 24 orientations, so there are 3,674,160 distinct states.

The canonical form picks one of the 24: the one with the yellow-orange-blue corner at down-left-back, yellow facing down. A solved cube then shows white on top and green in front. The graph grows with U, R and F turns only, which never move that corner, so every state in it is already canonical.

Facelet strings list the faces in the order U R F D L B, each read row by row as it appears in the net, with W Y R O G B for the colors.

Each cube in the graph is a real state, drawn with its actual stickers. Its distance from the center is the fewest turns needed to reach it. Small labels name the turn that leads outward along a link; click a cube and framed labels appear around it, one for each turn you can make from it (click one to make that turn).

Layers beyond 7 (5 with half turns) are drawn as dots, coloured from blue to pink by distance. Every layer sits on a sphere sized for its states, so the gaps between layers grow steadily from the centre. The first dot layer is evened out over its sphere once (layer 7 leaves it crowded on one side); after that, each dot layer is cut into small patches and the states that want a patch (next to the state they were reached from) are laid out inside it, at least 9 units apart. The sphere is made just large enough for the most crowded patch, so busy regions look dense and quiet regions sparse: that unevenness is real structure. Zoom toward any part (scroll with the pointer over it, pan with Space or Cmd/Ctrl + drag, or double-click) and the nearest dots, up to 20,000, become cubes with their real stickers; the closest also get their links and turn labels. Each state gets a number from its corner positions and twists, so the page can keep all 3,674,160 of them in a few small arrays. Click any cube or dot to select it: the cube jumps to that state and its route to solved lights up. Solve walks it back; Play from solved builds it up turn by turn.

The layers grow fast, then shrink: layer 11 holds 1,350,852 states, but only 276 states need the full 14 quarter turns. Those are the positions farthest from solved.

With quarter turns only, no link joins two states at the same distance: every quarter turn flips the corner parity. Switch on half turns and links inside a layer appear.
